Genetics and Molecular Biology

Genetics is the scientific study of genes, heredity, and variation in living organisms. Molecular biology examines the molecular mechanisms behind biological processes, particularly how DNA encodes and expresses genetic information.

DNA Structure
Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is the molecule that carries genetic information in all living organisms. DNA has a double helix structure, first described by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, based partly on X-ray crystallography data from Rosalind Franklin. Each strand consists of nucleotides, which are composed of a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate group, and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), and cytosine (C). The two strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs: A pairs with T, and G pairs with C. This complementarity is fundamental to DNA replication and information transfer.

DNA Replication
DNA replication is the process by which a cell duplicates its DNA before cell division, ensuring each daughter cell receives a complete copy of the genome. Replication is semi-conservative, meaning each new DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesized strand. The process begins at origins of replication, where the double helix unwinds. Helicase enzymes break hydrogen bonds between base pairs, while DNA polymerase enzymes add new nucleotides complementary to each template strand. Primase synthesizes short RNA primers to initiate synthesis. Because DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides in the 5' to 3' direction, one strand (leading strand) is synthesized continuously while the other (lagging strand) is synthesized in fragments called Okazaki fragments, later joined by DNA ligase.

Transcription: DNA to RNA
Transcription converts DNA information into RNA. RNA polymerase binds to promoter sequences upstream of a gene and unwinds the DNA double helix. It synthesizes a single-stranded messenger RNA (mRNA) molecule complementary to the template DNA strand, using ribonucleotides rather than deoxyribonucleotides, and substituting uracil (U) for thymine (T). In eukaryotes, the primary transcript (pre-mRNA) undergoes processing: a 5' cap is added, a poly-A tail is attached to the 3' end, and introns (non-coding sequences) are spliced out while exons (coding sequences) are joined together. The mature mRNA then exits the nucleus.

Translation: RNA to Protein
Translation converts mRNA sequences into proteins at ribosomes. Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules carry specific amino acids and recognize codons (three-nucleotide sequences) on mRNA through complementary anticodon sequences. Ribosomes move along mRNA, catalyzing peptide bond formation between amino acids brought by successive tRNA molecules. The process begins at a start codon (AUG, coding for methionine) and ends at stop codons. The resulting polypeptide chain folds into a three-dimensional protein structure that determines its function. The genetic code is nearly universal across all life forms, with 64 codons encoding 20 amino acids and stop signals.

Mutations and Genetic Variation
Mutations are changes in DNA sequence. Point mutations involve single nucleotide changes: substitutions, insertions, or deletions. Insertions or deletions of nucleotides can cause frameshift mutations that alter all downstream codons. Chromosomal mutations involve larger-scale changes including duplications, inversions, and translocations. Mutations can be caused by errors in DNA replication, exposure to mutagens like radiation or certain chemicals, or viral insertions. While many mutations are neutral or harmful, some confer advantages and are the raw material for evolution by natural selection.

CRISPR-Cas9 Gene Editing
CRISPR-Cas9 (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) is a revolutionary gene-editing technology adapted from a bacterial immune system. Guide RNA directs the Cas9 enzyme to a specific target DNA sequence, where Cas9 makes a precise double-stranded cut. The cell's own repair mechanisms then modify the DNA. CRISPR can delete, replace, or insert genetic sequences with unprecedented precision and ease. Applications include correcting genetic diseases like sickle cell anemia and beta-thalassemia, developing disease-resistant crops, and advancing cancer research. Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing CRISPR-Cas9.

Epigenetics
Epigenetics studies heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve changes to the DNA sequence itself. DNA methylation involves adding methyl groups to cytosines, typically silencing genes. Histone modification alters how tightly DNA is wrapped around histone proteins, affecting gene accessibility. Environmental factors including diet, stress, and toxin exposure can cause epigenetic changes. Some epigenetic modifications can be passed to offspring, influencing development and disease risk across generations.
